Plan an acoustic insulation project

Monoglass Insulation · Independent information website · Updated September 28, 2026

A clearer description of the noise problem is more useful than asking for the highest number on a product sheet.

Describe where the sound causes a problem

Write down where the sound begins and where it is unwanted. Is conversation difficult within a busy room? Is noise heard in an adjacent room? Does the concern involve a floor above or equipment in another area? Give the assessor examples and the times when the issue is most noticeable.

This first description helps keep the proposal focused. A quieter-feeling dining room and improved separation between occupied suites are not interchangeable briefs, even if both involve an insulation discussion.

Read product and assembly evidence separately

Monoglass publishes acoustic product information and a separate sound-rating guide with different wall and ceiling constructions. The published assembly entries vary with framing, facing and insulation configuration. Source: official sound-rating guides.

Practical implication: ask the project professional to identify the construction relevant to your building. Do not copy a rating from a different assembly into the quote simply because the same brand appears in both.

For an exposed ceiling, discuss appearance too

The manufacturer's Sonoglass page describes a spray-applied acoustic treatment intended to combine acoustic performance with a finished appearance, including irregular ceiling shapes. Source: Sonoglass Acoustic Treatment.

Give the contractor room dimensions or drawings if available, ceiling photographs, the intended use and a description of visible services. Ask what parts of the ceiling are included and how edges, fixtures and future access will be handled in the proposed scope.

Prepare questions that make proposals comparable

  • What is the defined acoustic objective for this space?
  • Which product or assembly documentation supports the proposal?
  • Who will decide whether the proposed treatment is sufficient?
  • What visible finish and sample are included?
  • How will other building work and occupancy affect scheduling?
  • Is any assessment or completion measurement included, or excluded?

Keep expectations in writing

A proposal should distinguish the contractor’s work from any investigation or design still required. If the contractor is pricing an architect's specification, include that reference. If the objective has not yet been assessed, record the next decision rather than treating a price as proof of a particular result.

Ask who you should contact if the agreed scope changes. Keep the accepted proposal, selected finish, relevant drawings and any supporting reports in one project folder. That gives the owner, contractor and design team the same information to work from.

Start with a useful enquiry

Include your city, building type, room use, a plain-language description of the concern and any existing reports. The proposal comparison guide can help you organize the response.
